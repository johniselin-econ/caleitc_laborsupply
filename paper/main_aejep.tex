\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

%% --- Paragraph 1: Motivation (phase-in as policy lever) ---
%% Tightened from original opening. Cut enumeration of programs.

There is a large and growing literature that shows that providing resources to low-income households with young children can both decrease child poverty in the short run and improve the longer-term outcomes of children across a range of measures \citep{duncan_roadmap_2019,page_new_2024}. Since the 1990s, two major tax-based safety net programs---the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) and the Child Tax Credit (CTC)---have aimed to support low-income families while minimizing labor supply distortions by phasing in benefits with earned income. As a result, non-workers and very-low-income families receive little-to-no benefit from these programs. Understanding how individuals respond to changes in the parameters governing the phase-in of these credits is critical to evaluating proposed reforms, yet less is known about this margin than about the extensive-margin effects of the federal EITC.

%% --- Paragraph 2: This paper + CalEITC + approach ---

In this paper, I provide evidence on the labor supply response of workers to changes in incentives at the very bottom of the earnings distribution using quasi-experimental evidence stemming from the implementation of the California Earned Income Tax Credit (CalEITC). Passed in 2015, the CalEITC was designed to provide larger transfers to very-low-income, predominantly part-time, workers with children who were eligible for very small benefit amounts from federal tax credits \citep{rueben_upward_2017}. The CalEITC nearly doubled the phase-in rate of the combined federal and California EITC schedule, meaning that workers could maximize the refundable tax credit at a much lower level of earnings than from the federal EITC alone, as shown in \Cref{fig:fig_sch}. I employ a triple-difference approach---comparing the employment of single women with and without children, inside and outside of California, before and after the CalEITC---which accounts for the concern that differential trends between parents and non-parents may violate the parallel trends assumption in simpler designs \citep{kleven_eitc_2024}.

%% --- Paragraph 3: Main results ---

Applying this approach to American Community Survey (ACS) data from 2012 through 2017, I find no change in overall employment following the introduction of the CalEITC. However, part-time work increases by approximately 3 percentage points, and full-time work decreases by a similar amount. These offsetting effects suggest that the CalEITC induced a shift from full-time to part-time work, equal to approximately 50,000 single mothers. This finding is robust to Synthetic Difference-in-Differences \citep{arkhangelsky_synthetic_2021}, a quadruple-difference using education as a fourth dimension, and Oster (2019) bounds on selection. A placebo test on college-educated women shows no effect on part-time work, and while there is some evidence of a smaller decline in full-time work in that sample, the quadruple-difference and Oster bounds confirm that the main results are not driven by a California-specific confounder. Using data on hours and weeks worked, I find that the response occurs entirely through a reduction in hours per week, not weeks per year.

%% --- Paragraph 4: Earnings + discrete labor supply ---

Both the welfare effects of this transition and the appropriate model of labor supply depend on the counterfactual income a full-time worker would have earned absent the CalEITC. I present evidence favoring a discrete labor supply interpretation \citep{kosonen_discrete_2023}: annual earnings fall by \$700--\$1,500, and the earnings distribution shifts downward in a manner inconsistent with a marginal hours adjustment. These patterns are more consistent with workers choosing among a limited set of work arrangements.

%% --- Paragraph 5: Heterogeneity ---

To explore mechanisms, I examine heterogeneity across household characteristics. The largest shift from full-time to part-time work occurs among women living in multi-adult households, suggesting that household composition affects a woman's ability to adjust labor supply. Notably, the response is not limited to mothers of very young children, ruling out a pure childcare story. Individual earnings fall while household income remains relatively stable, consistent with intra-household smoothing \citep{chiappori_collective_1992, blundell_collective_2005}.

%% --- Paragraph 6: Elasticities + MVPF ---

Using these results, I calculate mobility elasticities of between 1.2 and 1.9, which are large relative to prior federal EITC estimates but may reflect the unique ability of mothers in multi-adult households to respond to incentives. Following \cite{hendren_unified_2020}, I estimate a Marginal Value of Public Funds (MVPF) of between 0.7 and 0.9---well below the MVPF of 3.2 to 4.2 estimated for the federal EITC by \cite{bastian_eitc_2021}. This gap is driven primarily by reduced earnings from the shift to part-time work and interactions with the federal tax and transfer system.

%% --- Paragraph 7: Contribution (compressed) ---

This paper presents one of the first evaluations of the CalEITC. Previous work by \cite{neumark_too_2025} finds no change in overall employment---a result consistent with my findings. My paper is the first to document the compositional shift from full-time to part-time work and to provide an MVPF estimate for the program. It contributes to the emerging literature on discrete labor supply responses to tax changes \citep{kosonen_discrete_2023}, the broader literature on EITC labor supply effects \citep{eissa_labor_1996, meyer_welfare_2001, hoynes_effective_2018, bastian_rise_2020, kleven_eitc_2024}, and recent evidence on earnings-benefit decoupling from policies such as the fully refundable 2021 CTC \citep{enriquez_short-term_2023, goldin_child_2024} and unconditional cash transfers \citep{vivalt_employment_2024}.\footnote{This paper also connects to research on CalEITC take-up \citep{iselin_measuring_2023} and on the California Young Child Tax Credit \citep{goldin_child_2024, unrath_filing_2024}.}

%% --- Road map (2 sentences) ---

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section \ref{sec:background} describes the federal and California EITCs. Section \ref{sec:conceptual} outlines the conceptual framework. Section \ref{sec:emp_data} presents the data and empirical strategy. Section \ref{sec:results} shows the main results. Section \ref{sec:earnings} examines earnings effects and elasticities. Section \ref{sec:heterogeneity} explores heterogeneity by household characteristics. Section \ref{sec:mvpf} estimates the MVPF. Section \ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Policy Background} \label{sec:background}

%% --- Federal EITC (compressed) ---

The federal EITC is one of the largest means-tested cash transfer programs in the United States, providing \$64 billion in refundable tax credits to 31 million low-income workers in 2022 \citep{irs_statistics_2023}. A taxpayer's EITC benefit is primarily a function of earned income, with parameters varying by number of qualifying children and marital status. The credit phases in at a fixed rate, holds constant through a plateau region, and then phases out, as depicted in \Cref{fig:eitc_sch_2016_a}.\footnote{I report the same figures for 2015 and 2017 in \cref{fig:fig_appA_sch_15_17}. The CTC during this period provided a maximum credit of \$1,000 per child; the combined refundable schedule is shown in \Cref{fig:fig_appA_eitc_ctc}.}

%% --- CalEITC (compressed) ---

In June 2015, California introduced the CalEITC. Unlike most of the 31 other states with EITC supplements, California's credit was not a fixed percentage of the federal EITC.\footnote{Most states trade administrative simplicity for policy flexibility by providing a credit equal to a percentage of the federal credit \citep{rueben_upward_2017}.} The CalEITC consisted only of phase-in and phase-out regions, and eligibility was limited to individuals with earnings on the phase-in region of the federal EITC. The result is a joint federal--state schedule (the black line in \Cref{fig:eitc_sch_2016_a}) with a faster phase-in rate, meaning taxpayers could receive close to their maximum EITC at a lower level of earnings.

%% --- CalEITC changes + targeting ---

The CalEITC has undergone changes since its introduction \citep{davis_who_2019}. Prior to 2017, self-employment income was excluded from the CalEITC earnings definition; this was amended in 2017, when the eligibility range was also expanded (see \Cref{fig:eitc_sch_2017}). As shown in \Cref{fig:hist_a} and noted by \cite{rueben_upward_2017}, workers eligible for the largest CalEITC benefits are predominantly part-time workers. Single mothers are the group most likely to benefit, based on pre-treatment earnings distributions (\Cref{fig:hist_b}).

\section{Conceptual Framework} \label{sec:conceptual}

%% --- Setup (compressed) ---

I describe the margins along which taxpayers may adjust labor supply in response to the CalEITC. Because the CalEITC mirrors federal EITC eligibility rules and nearly all CalEITC claimants also claim the federal EITC, the combined federal--state schedule is the relevant budget set \citep{davis_who_2019, iselin_measuring_2023}. As shown in \Cref{fig:budget_constr}, introducing the CalEITC shifts the budget constraint outward.

%% --- Continuous model (compressed) ---

Following \cite{kosonen_discrete_2023}, consider an agent with utility $u(c,z)$, where $c$ is after-tax income and $z$ is gross income. With a continuous choice set, the budget constraint is $c = z(1-\tau) + R$, where $(1-\tau)$ is the net-of-tax rate and $R$ is virtual income. Under the CalEITC, agents in region A of \Cref{fig:budget_constr} face competing substitution and income effects, while agents in region B have an unambiguous incentive to work less. Past work has generally found that agents respond along the extensive rather than intensive margin \citep{saez_taxpayers_2010, chetty_adjustment_2011, nichols_earned_2015, kleven_eitc_2024}. Importantly, under continuous labor supply, agents in region C---the area to the left of the federal EITC kink---would not be expected to respond.

%% --- Discrete model (compressed) ---

Now consider a discrete model, following \cite{saez_optimal_2002} and \cite{kosonen_discrete_2023}, in which agents choose among $J$ discrete levels of earnings. In a simple example, agents choose between not working, part-time, and full-time work, as denoted by the dashed lines on \Cref{fig:budget_constr}. This setup still allows the extensive-margin response seen in the continuous model, but it also rationalizes larger shifts from full-time to part-time work among agents who were previously in region C---a response that the continuous model cannot explain.

\section{Empirical Strategy and Data} \label{sec:emp_data}

\subsection{Empirical Strategy} \label{sec:emp}

%% --- Triple-diff design (compressed) ---

My main specification is a cross-sectional triple-difference design, comparing single women with and without qualifying children (difference 1), before and after the CalEITC in 2015 (difference 2), in California versus untreated states (difference 3). This estimate recovers a causal effect under the assumption that the employment difference between single women with and without children in California would have trended similarly to that difference in control states \citep{olden_triple_2022}. The approach leverages two untreated groups---non-parents and out-of-state parents---and controls for economic and demographic trends within California that affect both groups equally. I estimate:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eq1}
\begin{split}
       y_{ist} = &  \alpha + \beta \text{Treated}_{ist} + \eta  X_{it} + \theta( U_{st} \times \text{QC}_{it}) + \mu(\text{MW}_{st} \times \text{QC}_{it}) \\
       & + \delta_t + \gamma_s + \text{QC}_{it} + (\text{QC} \times \delta)_{it} + (\text{QC}\times \gamma)_{ist} + (\delta \times \gamma)_{st} + \epsilon_{it}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

where $y_{ist}$ is the outcome for individual $i$ in state $s$ at time $t$, $\text{QC}_{it}$ is a fixed effect for the number of qualifying children (capped at 3), and the remaining terms include state, year, and all pairwise interaction fixed effects. Treatment is defined as:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eq2}
\text{Treated}_{ist} = \textbf{1} \left [ (\text{QC}_{it} > 0) \And (\text{t} \geq 2015) \And (\text{s} = \text{CA})  \right ]
\end{equation}
%
Standard errors are clustered at the state level.\footnote{With one treated cluster among 28, standard CRVE may over-reject. In \Cref{sec:app_inf}, I implement alternative inference procedures including randomization inference, wild-cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron_bootstrap-based_2008}, and the correction of \cite{ferman_inference_2019}. The part-time estimates remain at or near the ten percent level under the wild-cluster bootstrap ($p = 0.10$--$0.12$ across specifications) and significant at the five percent level under the \cite{ferman_inference_2019} correction in the preferred specification; the full-time estimates are significant only under the latter correction, and the employment estimates are indistinguishable from zero under all procedures. Conley--Taber (2011) confidence intervals, which use only the across-state placebo distribution, are wide and include zero for all outcomes.}

%% --- Controls (compressed) ---

The vector $X_{it}$ includes demographic controls (age, education, race, Hispanic origin, age of youngest child, number of adults in household). I also control for state unemployment $\times$ QC count and binding state minimum wage $\times$ QC count, to account for differential effects of labor market conditions and minimum wage changes across family types.\footnote{California began a series of minimum wage increases in 2014, coinciding with the CalEITC. \cite{godoy_parental_2024} find that single mothers respond to minimum wage increases by increasing full-time employment, implying that without this control, my estimates of the CalEITC's full-time effect would be biased toward zero.}

%% --- Control states (compressed) ---

My control state pool excludes Alaska and states with EITC policy changes between 2008 and 2019, yielding 28 states including California (\Cref{tab:state_tab}).\footnote{Excluded states: CO, CT, HI, IL, IA, KS, LA, ME, MD, MA, MI, MN, MT, NJ, NM, OH, OR, RI, SC, VT, WI.} I perform robustness checks with alternative control pools, including restricting to states without any state EITC and to Medicaid expansion states.

%% --- Event study ---

I extend the analysis to an event-study specification:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eq3}
\begin{split}
       y_{ist} = &  \alpha + \sum_{j \neq 2014} \beta_j \left ( \text{Year}_{j=t} \times \textbf{1} \left [ (\text{QC}_{it} > 0) \And (\text{s} = \text{CA})  \right ] \right ) \\
       & + \eta X_{it}  + \theta ( U_{st} \times \text{QC}_{it})+ \mu (\text{MW}_{st} \times \text{QC}_{it}) \\
       & + \delta_t + \gamma_s + \text{QC}_{it} + (\text{QC} \times \delta)_{it} + (\text{QC}\times \gamma)_{ist} + (\delta \times \gamma)_{st} + \epsilon_{it}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

My main specification covers 2012 through 2017 to avoid the Great Recession recovery and post-2017 confounders (TCJA, YCTC).

%% --- SDID (compressed) ---

To address the concern that control states may not approximate California's counterfactual trends, I supplement the triple-difference with Synthetic Difference-in-Differences \citep{arkhangelsky_synthetic_2021}. I estimate SDID using a county-year panel, constructing a synthetic version of each California county and reporting population-weighted average effects.\footnote{I adapt the bootstrap procedure in \cite{clarke_synthetic_2023} for standard errors.}


\subsection{Data} \label{sec:data}

%% --- ACS + sample (compressed) ---

My primary data source is the American Community Survey (ACS) from 2010 through 2017, downloaded via IPUMS \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023}. My main outcome variables are indicators for employment during the last 12 months, and for full-time (35+ hours/week) versus part-time ($<$35 hours/week) work, following the BLS definition.\footnote{The ACS reference period is the 12 months prior to interview, not the calendar year. I address potential timing concerns in \Cref{sec:app_a} using CPS monthly data.}

My main sample consists of single women aged 20--49 without a college degree, who are US citizens, not in school, not in group quarters or the armed services, and not potential qualifying children. I assign qualifying children to adults using household composition and demographics, abstracting from strategic claiming behavior \citep{jones_doubled-up_2016}; any resulting mis-assignment attenuates my estimates. Balance tests (\Cref{tab:tab_appA_balance_test_3d_basic,tab:tab_appA_balance_test_3d_controls}) show small demographic differences that are controlled for in my main specification.

\subsection{Decomposition} \label{sec:decomposition}

%% --- Hours bins ---

I decompose the shift by hours worked per week in \Cref{fig:fig_hours}. Employment declines among women working 40--60 hours per week and increases among those working 20--34 hours, consistent with a substantial reduction in average hours rather than a marginal adjustment near the full-time threshold.

%% FIGURE 5: Hours bins

\subsection{Threats to Identification} \label{sec:threats}

The estimates above could in principle reflect other California-specific shocks that coincided with the CalEITC's introduction and differentially affected single mothers without a college degree. Three candidates stand out: the state's minimum wage increases, the Medicaid expansion, and a broader California-specific trend in mothers' employment. I address each with a targeted test.

%% NOTE: add cengiz_effect_2019 (Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, Zipperer, QJE 2019) and
%% jardim_minimum_2022 (Jardim et al., AEJ:EP 2022) to references.bib on Overleaf.

\paragraph{Minimum wage.} California raised its statewide minimum wage from \$8.00 to \$9.00 in July 2014, to \$10.00 in January 2016, and to \$10.50 in January 2017, and several large cities---San Francisco, San Jose, Los Angeles, and Oakland among them---enacted higher local ordinances over the same window. The main specifications control for the binding state minimum wage $\times$ QC count, but the direction of any remaining bias is theoretically ambiguous: \cite{godoy_parental_2024} find that minimum wage increases raise single mothers' full-time employment, which would bias the full-time estimate toward zero, while the hours-adjustment literature finds that employers respond to minimum wage increases in part by reducing hours \citep{cengiz_effect_2019, jardim_minimum_2022}---a response that could mimic the shift from full-time to part-time work that I attribute to the credit.

I separate the two policies using cross-county variation within California, which is immune by construction to concerns about the out-of-state or college-educated comparison groups. From the 2012--2014 pre-period I construct, for each of the 35 treated county units of \Cref{tab:sdid}, a minimum wage \emph{bite} measure---the share of employed workers in the analysis sample whose implied hourly wage falls below the incoming \$10.50 statewide minimum---and a CalEITC \emph{exposure} measure---the share of mothers with positive earnings at or below the credit's maximum qualifying income. Counties differ widely on both measures (bite: 0.22--0.55; exposure: 0.09--0.30), and the two are only moderately correlated (0.53), so the data can distinguish where each policy binds. I then regress the within-county difference in outcomes between mothers and non-mothers on the interactions of a post-2015 indicator with both standardized measures.

Panel A of \Cref{tab:mw_bite} shows that the part-time increase tracks the credit, not the minimum wage: a one-standard-deviation increase in CalEITC exposure raises the part-time effect by 1.5 percentage points ($p = 0.053$), while the minimum wage bite coefficient is negative---the opposite sign of what minimum-wage-driven hours reductions would produce. The full-time decline likewise loads on exposure rather than on bite, which is again wrong-signed for a minimum wage confounder, although neither full-time coefficient is precisely estimated with 35 clusters. Panel B addresses the local ordinances directly by re-estimating the weighted SDID of \Cref{tab:sdid} excluding the ordinance counties. The part-time effect is essentially unchanged (3.4 and 2.9 versus 3.5 percentage points in the full sample), while the full-time decline falls by roughly half and loses statistical significance. Because Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Santa Clara together account for about a third of the treated-sample population (Los Angeles alone, 28 percent), this comparison also changes the estimand; I therefore read it as evidence that the full-time decline is concentrated in the large urban counties---where the credit and the ordinances both bind---rather than as a clean attribution. Together, the two panels support the bounded interpretation in \Cref{sec:decomposition}: the part-time increase is robustly attributable to the CalEITC, while part of the full-time decline may reflect concurrent minimum wage increases.

\subsection{Elasticities} \label{sec:elasticity}

To contextualize these results, I construct ``mobility'' elasticities that capture the degree to which workers relocate across the earnings distribution in response to changes in average tax rates \citep{saez_optimal_2002, kreiner_taxation_2015, kosonen_discrete_2023}. This requires assuming that the entire part-time increase comes from former full-time workers, and specifying counterfactual full-time earnings. Given uncertainty about the counterfactual, I consider three levels: annual minimum-wage earnings (\$21,840), and median and mean 2014 full-time earnings of California single mothers (\$30,655 and \$36,413 in 2017 dollars).

I find mobility elasticities of 1.2, 1.7, and 1.9 for each assumption, respectively. These are large relative to prior federal EITC estimates, exceeding all but those from the 1986 reform \citep{kleven_eitc_2024}. However, the group driving the response---single mothers in multi-adult households---may be uniquely able to adjust labor supply relative to the populations studied in earlier work.

\subsection{Household Composition} \label{sec:het_adults}

I examine heterogeneity by the number of adults in the household. As shown in \Cref{fig:het_adults}, the shift from full-time to part-time work is concentrated among households with three or more adults: part-time work increases by approximately 5 percentage points, and full-time work decreases by approximately 7 percentage points. Among households with one or two adults, the effects are smaller and not always statistically significant. This concentration in larger households points to a role for household-level decision-making in the response to the CalEITC, with implications for the literature on collective labor supply \citep{chiappori_collective_1992, blundell_collective_2005}. The most straightforward explanation is that it is less costly for single mothers to reduce labor supply when other earners are present in the household.

\section{MVPF and Fiscal Externalities} \label{sec:mvpf}

I estimate the Marginal Value of Public Funds (MVPF) of the CalEITC following \cite{hendren_unified_2020}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mvpf}
    \text{MVPF} = \frac{\text{Willingness-to-Pay}}{\text{Program Cost}}
\end{equation}

I estimate willingness-to-pay by assuming taxpayers value \$1 in refundable credit at \$1, and, via the envelope theorem, that workers who change labor supply are approximately indifferent between their new and counterfactual work arrangements.\footnote{This assumption is non-trivial; see \cite{lockwood_optimal_2020} for discussion of settings where the behavioral response itself has welfare value.} Therefore WTP $= \text{CalEITC}_D - \text{CalEITC}_B$, where the subscripts denote the direct program cost and the cost attributable to behavioral responses, respectively.

Program cost incorporates fiscal spillovers from the labor supply response on federal and state tax revenue:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\text{Program Cost} & = \text{CalEITC}_D - \text{Federal IIT Tax Changes} \\
  & - \text{Federal Payroll Tax Changes} - \text{California IIT Tax Changes}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

I simulate fiscal spillovers using TAXSIM, drawing a random sample of single mothers working part-time in the CalEITC earnings range and computing their federal and state tax liabilities with and without the labor supply change.\footnote{I do not estimate spillovers from other social programs or long-term effects due to data limitations; the direction of bias is ambiguous. See Online Appendix for details.}

%% --- Results ---

Across all specifications and counterfactual earnings assumptions, I estimate a MVPF of between 0.7 and 0.9 (\Cref{fig:fig_hist_2017_f1_mvpf}), implying it costs the government between \$1.10 and \$1.60 to deliver \$1 in benefits. The MVPF is lower when the assumed counterfactual full-time income is higher, as the federal government loses more revenue when a higher earner reduces labor supply. The fiscal cost is driven primarily by reduced payroll tax revenue. For higher counterfactual earnings, the loss is compounded by increased federal EITC eligibility as workers move back onto the phase-out region.\footnote{The decomposition of fiscal spillovers by revenue source is shown in \Cref{fig:appA_fiscal_spill}.}

These estimates are well below the MVPF of 3.2 to 4.2 estimated by \cite{bastian_eitc_2021} for the federal EITC, a gap driven by the earnings reduction from the full-time to part-time shift and its interaction with federal taxes. This highlights that the net fiscal cost of state-level programs can depend critically on how they interact with federal policies.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, I examine the labor supply response of single mothers to the implementation of the CalEITC. Using a triple-difference design, supported by Synthetic Difference-in-Differences, I find that the CalEITC shifted single mothers from full-time to part-time work between 2015 and 2017. Annual earnings fell by roughly \$700--\$1{,}500, while overall employment is essentially unchanged; the compositional shift operates primarily through hours per week rather than weeks per year.

The estimates are robust across specifications, including a quadruple-difference using education as a fourth dimension and Oster (2019) bounds on selection, both of which confirm that the results are not driven by unobserved confounders. The results are more consistent with a discrete, rather than continuous, labor supply model: workers appear to respond by switching among a limited set of work arrangements. Using the estimated behavioral responses, I obtain mobility elasticities between 1.2 and 1.9 and an MVPF between 0.7 and 0.9, implying that the program costs more than \$1 in (state plus federal) revenue to deliver \$1 in benefits to recipients.

I also find that the full-time to part-time transition is concentrated among large, multi-adult households, indicating an important role for intra-household bargaining in allowing women the flexibility to respond to the CalEITC's incentives. Individual earnings fall while household income remains relatively stable, consistent with intra-household smoothing. These findings suggest that household context matters for understanding behavioral responses to tax policy: characteristics outside the tax unit but inside the household appear to inform the elasticity of a given worker.

Two important limitations stem from the ACS data. First, without longitudinal data, I cannot track individual transitions or the duration of part-time work spells. Second, self-reported earnings are a noisy measure of true taxable earnings, making it difficult to pin down the counterfactual income of switchers---a key parameter for welfare analysis. Both limitations could be addressed with administrative earnings records that match the tax-year reference period and allow direct observation of earnings transitions. Ideally, such data would also capture utilization of other safety net programs, enabling a more complete MVPF estimate.

\section{Alternative Inference} \label{sec:app_inf}

The main estimates cluster standard errors at the state level with a single treated cluster (California) among 28 states, a setting in which standard cluster-robust inference can over-reject \citep{ferman_inference_2019}. \Cref{tab:tab_appE_inference} re-evaluates the main triple-difference estimates under six alternative procedures: a wild-cluster bootstrap \citep[WCBS;][]{cameron_bootstrap-based_2008}; randomization-inference wild bootstrap p-values based on the t-statistic (RIWB-t) and on the coefficient (RIWB-b), which compare the observed statistic to a placebo distribution generated by reassigning treatment among the never-treated control states; a pairs block bootstrap resampling states (BB); the \cite{ferman_inference_2019} correction to the block bootstrap (Corrected BB), which adjusts the bootstrap distribution for the single treated cluster; and pure randomization inference (RI), which re-estimates the actual model with treatment reassigned to each of the 27 control states and compares the observed coefficient to the placebo distribution. The table also reports Conley--Taber (2011) 90 percent confidence intervals, constructed by inverting that placebo-coefficient distribution.

The employment estimates are indistinguishable from zero under every procedure. The part-time estimates are stable near the ten percent level under the wild-cluster bootstrap across all four specifications and reach the five percent level under the Ferman--Pinto correction in the preferred specification. The full-time estimates are significant only under the Ferman--Pinto correction; wild-cluster bootstrap and randomization-inference p-values for full-time work lie above conventional thresholds. The pure placebo-refit procedures are the least powerful, as they draw on nothing beyond the 27 placebo states' coefficient distribution, whose dispersion is an order of magnitude larger than the CRVE standard errors: the RI p-values exceed conventional thresholds and the Conley--Taber 90 percent intervals include zero for every outcome and specification. I read these as bounding exercises: the design cannot distinguish the estimates from state-level shocks of the size observed among control states, while the procedures that exploit within-state variation place the part-time result at or below the ten percent level.
